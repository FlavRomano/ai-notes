SRM uses VC-dim as a 
- controlling parameter
- for minimizing the generalization bound on $R$ (true risk)

So it helps us to choose the best model for generalization 

Assuming finite $VC$ 
- we can define a **nested structure** of models-hypothesis space
- according to the VC-dim $$\begin{align}
\mathcal{H_{1}} \subseteq \mathcal{H_{2}} \subseteq &\dots \subseteq  \mathcal{H}_{n} \\
VC(\mathcal{H_{1}}) \leq &\dots \leq VC(\mathcal{H}_{n})
\end{align}
$$

![[Pasted image 20251031175049.png]]

Formally SRM exploits the VC-bound $$R \leq R_{emp} + \underbrace{\varepsilon\left( \frac{1}{l}, VC, \frac{1}{\delta} \right)}_{VC-\text{confidence}}$$
aims to find the **best trade-off** between the two opposing forces in the bound, which is illustrated as a U-shaped plot.

1. **Low Complexity (Underfitting):** If the model is too simple (low VC-dim), the $R_{emp}$​ (training error) is high.

2. **High Complexity (Overfitting):** If the model is too complex (high VC-dim), the $R_{emp}$​ is low (possibly zero on training data), but the $\varepsilon$ term increases, causing the guaranteed risk ($R$) to rise. 

The optimal choice of model complexity (the structure) is the one that provides the minimum of the expected risk (R) bound. SRM is thus considered the **inductive principle** based on the control of complexity.

> remark: that VC-confidence measures the **cost of complexity**.
> - increases with complexity
> - shrinks if $l$ gets bigger
>	- more training data
