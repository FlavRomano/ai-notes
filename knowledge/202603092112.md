> **Reichenbach's Common Cause Principle** is a foundational concept in causality that formally connects 
> - statistical correlation (what we observe in data) 
> - with causation (the underlying physical or logical mechanism).

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two variables such that are statistically dependent. $$X \not\perp Y$$ 
Then it holds
1. $X$ is **indirectly causing** $Y$ or
2. $Y$ is **indirectly causing** $X$ or
3. there is a possibly **unobserved common cause** $Z$ that indirectly causes both $X$ and $Y$

![[Drawing 2026-03-09 21.15.09.excalidraw]]

In other words: if two variables are statistically dependent, there must be a causal explanation for it.

> From the prospective of "*correlation does not imply causation*" Reichenbach principle does a step further.
> It states that correlation 
> - **does not imply** direct causation between the two variables 
> - but it **does imply** that a causal relationship **exists somewhere** in the system.

Unfortunately this principle comes with strong assumptions: assumes that we can **perfectly identify true statistical dependence** from our data. That's something very difficult in the real world.
