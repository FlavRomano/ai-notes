> The ATE is the standard metric used in science and causal inference to measure the **true causal impact** of a decision, treatment, or policy on an outcome across a population.
> - does smoking cause cancer?
> - will a vaccine prevent long-term infection?
>
> we want to know the **difference** in the outcome if we apply the treatment versus if we do not.

For a binary treatment variable $Y_{1}$ and an outcome variable $Y_2$ the ATE is defined as $$ATE(Y_{1}, Y_{2)} = \mathbb{E}[Y_{2}\mid do(Y_{1} := True)] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{2}\mid do(Y_{1}:=False)]$$ e.g $Y_{1}=True$ is taking a drug, $Y_{1}=False$ is taking a placebo, and $Y_2$ is the recovery.

In standard conditioning when we calculate the difference in conditional expectations we measure the **correlation** $$\mathbb{E}[Y_{2}\mid Y_{1} = True] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{2}\mid Y_{1} = False]$$ 

>e.g people who voluntarily take a vaccine might also have better access to healthcare, their better recovery rates might be due to their baseline health, not the vaccine itself.

To find the **true causal effect** we have to simulate a scenario where we force the treatment. As a matter of fact $do(Y_{1} := True)$ cut the arrows from all background confounders.
